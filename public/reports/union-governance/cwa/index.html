The parent union

The Communications Workers of America (CWA) is a multi-industry international union organized through geographic districts, industrial sectors and divisions, and chartered locals. Its Constitution assigns different powers to members, Convention delegates, the Executive Board, officers and subordinate bodies. Affiliation and merger agreements can preserve different rights for particular sectors.

This analysis examines the parent union’s governance and finances: who makes decisions, how members exercise control, how dues and other funds are reported, and what the available records establish. The CWA-AFA sector analysis addresses the flight-attendant organization within that wider framework.

Sources: Constitution, Articles VII–XIII and IX.6; current CWA leadership roster.

Principal findings

  • Convention authority is subject to member referendum. The Executive Board acts between conventions, and the President executes established policy within constitutional limits.
  • Different offices have different electorates. Local members elect officers and Convention delegates by secret ballot. District delegates elect district vice presidents.
  • Districts, sectors, locals and federal reporting entities describe different things. An internal governance unit should not automatically be treated as a separate treasury.
  • Financial totals need their reporting boundaries. Parent, sector and local amounts cannot be combined without reconciling transfers. Reported spending categories do not establish the quantity or quality of service delivered to an individual member.

Sources: Constitution, VII.1, XI, XIII.9 and XV; financial analysis and source-status register.

CWA-AFA: the merger-benefit question

What benefit did the 2004-era merger bring AFA, and what identifiable legal, financial or advisory support did CWA provide afterward? The sector review follows that question through the merger obligations, actual service evidence, financial flows and member outcomes.

Access to a parent’s resources, a recorded payment and a demonstrated member benefit are different findings. The review needs to establish the service, the provider, the beneficiary, who paid, whether reimbursement occurred, and the outcome that can be supported. The merger agreement’s reserved rights also matter when deciding which organization controlled the work.
