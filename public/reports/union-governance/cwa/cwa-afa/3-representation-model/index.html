Analysis: how representation is organized

CWA-AFA assigns representation across local, airline and sector bodies. Local officers handle council business and member concerns; the MEC coordinates the airline; sector officers, staff and governing boards provide the wider framework. Airline agreements and committee rules determine the detailed path for a particular dispute.

Sources: Constitution and Bylaws, III.C/E and VII.A–C, physical pp.18–23,38–40; official airline contract directory.

Local Councils and Local Executive Councils

The Local Council is the membership forum. Its elected President, Vice President and Secretary serve in the LEC. Councils may elect additional Local Council Representatives: up to two for councils with 2,000 or fewer members, with one further representative allowed for each additional 1,000 members or portion thereof. The decision to elect or reduce these additional positions is made at a regular membership meeting with the business on its advance agenda.

The Local Council President is responsible for timely processing of members’ complaints and grievances and for implementing local, airline and sector policies. Article III also requires officers to be contactable and responsive. These are assigned duties; the document alone does not demonstrate how promptly an individual case was handled.

Sources: Constitution and Bylaws, III.C.1 and III.C.3(a), physical pp.18–19.

MECs and airline-wide representation

At an airline with multiple Local Councils, their presidents form the MEC. For a single-council airline, the LEC also serves as the MEC. The MEC establishes airline policy, coordinates councils, elects specified officers and System Board members, maintains records and reports to members. Routine local business remains with Local Councils.

The MEC President is the airline’s union spokesperson and chief executive officer, with grievance and managerial duties. Officer status and voting status must be distinguished: a MEC officer who is not a Local Council President does not automatically have the same vote as a council representative. The elections page explains the voting and substitution rules.

Sources: Constitution and Bylaws, VII.A–D, physical pp.38–43.

Specialized committees: a sourced airline example

United’s published committee descriptions illustrate how local and airline work connect. Its MEC Grievance Committee coordinates MEC grievances, works with staff attorneys and local grievance representatives, and distributes settlement and System Board decisions. Its MEC Reserve Committee assists local reserve committees and works with the grievance committee on system-wide reserve violations.

These are United-specific examples. They identify assigned work and coordination; they are not a finding that every airline uses identical committees or that every dispute follows the same procedure.

Sources: United MEC Grievance Committee, duties; United MEC Reserve Committee, duties.

Sector staff and identifiable parent support

The sector’s official bargaining description pairs elected flight-attendant committees with professional negotiators. The merger agreement also provides for parent professional assistance and preserves sector legal responsibilities. To assess a particular service, the analysis must distinguish sector staff, parent departments and outside providers.

Sources: Official contracts page, professional negotiators and elected committees; Constitution and Bylaws, Appendix A §§7–8,11, physical pp.162–163.

A named contemporary example is SIF 726. CWA’s 2025 oversight report describes a funded corporate researcher supporting United bargaining, Delta organizing and regional boarding pay. The public summary does not allocate the project’s cost among those assignments. The financial analysis examines the service and payment trail without assuming that sector-paid work is an additional parent subsidy.

Sources: 2025 Defense Fund Oversight Committee report, physical pp.4,8.

Records that connect structure to service

The constitutional reporting duties provide specific records to examine: the airline’s annual report, MEC expenditure statements, council meeting minutes and relevant decisions. For an assessment of service delivery, useful evidence also includes case counts, disposition times, settlements, staffing assignments and member communications. Those outcome measures must be evaluated alongside costs and workload, rather than inferred from an organizational chart.

Sources: Constitution and Bylaws, III.D.9; VII.B.1(h–i), VII.C.2(c) and VII.D.8, physical pp.22,38–43.

Significance

The structure creates local points of contact and airline-wide coordination, with sector and parent resources available under their respective rules. Whether those arrangements deliver value is an empirical question. This analysis identifies duties and documented assistance while keeping service quality, funding source and net benefit as separate matters to verify.
