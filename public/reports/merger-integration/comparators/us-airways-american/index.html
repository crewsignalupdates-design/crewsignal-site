Historical Comparator Report

US Airways / American

Bankruptcy legacies, representation handoff, transition mechanics, and the path to the New American flight-attendant JCBA.

This report is informational and comparative only. It does not replace governing contract text, side letters, past practice, arbitration awards, or official union/company interpretations.

About this comparison. This comparison follows the agreements governing legacy US Airways (LUS) and legacy American Airlines (LAA) flight attendants into the merger, through the December 2014 joint contract, and into later implementation. The principal sources are signed agreements, contract text, the arbitration award, government records, and contemporary union and company accounts. The award, later wage agreements, and operational changes took effect on different dates.

Overview

Both flight-attendant groups entered the US Airways / American merger with contracts shaped by earlier restructuring. LUS brought the combined US Airways / CWA-AFA Red Book, ratified February 28, 2013, after years of separate America West and US Airways East contract administration. LAA brought the 2001 APFA / American agreement as modified by the 2003 Restructuring Participation Agreement, the 2012 bankruptcy agreement, and the Conditional Labor Agreement. The joint bargaining process had to reconcile those terms while some predecessor rules remained in operation during transition.

Representation agreements, transition rules, a fixed bargaining protocol, mediation, and binding interest arbitration established the path to a joint collective bargaining agreement. Existing legacy American plan participants entered the merger with accrued benefits under a frozen company-sponsored defined-benefit plan. Eligible former US Airways East participants carried benefits under a terminated plan administered by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC); former America West employees had a different, defined-contribution retirement history. These inherited differences survived the creation of a common employment agreement. The plan outcomes do not, by themselves, establish how much responsibility belonged to union strategy, management, PBGC, or the bankruptcy process. LAA retirement letter; PBGC plan record; America West 401(k) filing.

Different starting positions. American flight attendants accepted concessions in 2003 and during Chapter 11. Eligible LAA pension participants retained accrued benefits in a frozen company plan. Eligible former US Airways East participants instead had benefits administered by PBGC after termination of their plan. Former America West employees had a different retirement history. The common contract brought wage and work-rule changes without removing those inherited differences.

Seniority integration in one view

APFA and CWA-AFA jointly designed the Flight Attendant seniority process used in the American / US Airways merger. The protocol combined American Occupational Seniority Dates with legacy US Airways Seniority Integration Dates, reconciled specified training-credit differences, and preserved the internal order of each predecessor list. The legacy US Airways input list already contained adjustments from the earlier America West / US Airways integration, so the result was not a raw company-date-of-hire sort. Signed Seniority Integration Protocol.

Representation transfer, list integration, JCBA bargaining, JCBA effectiveness, and common operational implementation remained separate milestones. The parties’ Agreement on Bargaining and Representation also preserved equal legacy participation in joint bargaining and continuing CWA-AFA responsibilities during the handoff to APFA certification.

Transition from the LUS Red Book into the American merger

The America West / US Airways JCBA story ends with the 2013 LUS Red Book. It unified the former US Airways East and America West flight-attendant groups under one CWA-AFA agreement after years of separate East/West contract administration and failed or delayed parity efforts. Its supersession provisions retained specified letters and implementation arrangements, including predecessor rules that continued during transition.

The Red Book established a combined wage and work-rule structure after the earlier East-side restructuring agreements and the separate America West contract. At the December 2013 American merger, the Red Book and its implementation letters governed LUS. Earlier East and West provisions remained relevant where the transition arrangements expressly continued them.

Legacy American / APFA baseline before bankruptcy

Before the 2003 restructuring, APFA had negotiated a new agreement through a Railway Labor Act campaign. Its 2000–2001 communications describe member surveys, InfoRep expansion, informational picketing, strike-ballot education, mediation explanations, and preparation for possible lawful self-help. APFA's September 2000 Skyword explained the path from mediation to a possible NMB proffer of binding arbitration, rejection of arbitration, a 30-day cooling-off period, and then self-help if no agreement was reached. Skyword, Vol. 3, Issue 7 (September 2000), pp. 4–5.

The same record shows APFA using the memory of the 1993 strike as leverage. Member-facing articles described systemwide picketing, strike ballot results, and a strategy of demonstrating unity to American, the NMB, and the public. Skyword, Vol. 4, Issue 4; Skyword, Vol. 4, Issue 1. APFA presented the resulting 2001 agreement to members as an improvement secured through that bargaining campaign.

APFA's post-ratification materials described the 2001 agreement as ratified with 96 percent voting in favor, following a 96 percent strike-authorization vote earlier that year. The union also reported that more than 100 contract changes were being implemented, including changes to compensation, expenses, retirement, vacation, crew meals, Personal Vacation Days (PVDs), crew rooms, training rooms, per diem, and scheduling-related provisions. Skyword, Vol. 4, Issue 7; Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 2.

Retirement was a central part of that baseline. APFA communications described Article 36 retirement improvements and later retirement outlines used by the union distinguished among Article 30 benefits, age 50–55 rules, age 55 retiree status, age 55 retiree/pensioner status, and age 60 retiree/pensioner status. The chart tied pension eligibility to credited service, retiree medical and travel to company seniority and age, and defined the retirement formula around Final Average Earnings and Years of Credited Service. APFA retirement outline.

American after September 11 and the TWA acquisition

The 2001 agreement immediately entered an extraordinary operating environment. The ratification result was counted on September 12, 2001, one day after the terrorist attacks involving American Airlines flights 11 and 77. APFA's later account explained that the contract had been ratified with approximately 96 percent voting in favor, but that the news was overtaken by the events of September 11. Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 9. APFA's special September 11 and Flight 587 editions document the operational, emotional, safety, and communications burdens that followed. Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 8; Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 1.

The same period also included the TWA transaction. In May/June 2002, APFA published the NMB certification history and described the TWA-LLC representation sequence. APFA reported that the NMB determined American and TWA-LLC were a single transportation system for representation purposes, that APFA replaced IAM for the TWA-LLC flight attendants, and that APFA then represented roughly 27,000 flight attendants at American. Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 5; Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 3.

By 2002, APFA was also dealing with furloughs, overage leaves, and recall efforts. In July 2002, APFA reported 1,566 furloughed members, comprising 733 American and 833 former TWA-LLC flight attendants, and 413 recalls through an overage-leave settlement. Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 6, p. 4. Later that year, APFA reported that additional overages and furloughs brought the total number of APFA flight attendants on furlough to 2,124. Skyword, Vol. 5, Issue 10.

Conditions before the 2003 concessions. The September 11 attacks, Flight 587, TWA-LLC integration, furloughs, and American's financial deterioration coincided with implementation of the recently ratified agreement. APFA negotiated the 2003 concessions in that setting.

American's 2003 restructuring agreement

The 2003 APFA / American Restructuring Participation Agreement was a bankruptcy-avoidance concession package. APFA's January and February 2003 communications show the escalation: American first asked APFA to forego the January 1, 2003 three percent pay increase and July 1, 2003 premium-pay increase; then American demanded $1.8 billion in annual labor-cost reductions, including $340 million from flight attendants. APFA reported that it retained a financial advisor, reactivated its negotiating committee, met with management, and adopted a readiness plan that required membership approval before relief would be provided. Skyword, Vol. 6, Issue 1 (January 2003), pp. 4–5; Skyword, Vol. 6, Issue 2 (February 2003), pp. 3–4.

By March and April 2003, the pressure had become explicit. APFA's March issue reproduced Board language authorizing leadership to reach a negotiated agreement to avoid a possible bankruptcy. The April/May issue then reported that American imposed a March 31 agreement deadline and insisted that ratification be completed by April 15 or the company would file for bankruptcy. APFA used a 15-day telephonic vote rather than its normal 30-day mail-ballot procedure because the company's bankruptcy deadline would expire first. Skyword, Vol. 6, Issue 3; Skyword, Vol. 6, Issue 4.

The ratification deadline was extended to April 16. APFA reported 10,761 votes in favor and 9,652 against, a 52.72 percent approval among 20,413 valid ballots. After disclosures concerning executive compensation and benefits, the APFA Board directed a reballot on April 22. Following further negotiations, the Board withdrew that instruction and accepted additional changes without another membership vote. APFA's contemporary account described both the internal dispute and the company's renewed bankruptcy deadline. Skyword, Vol. 6, Issue 4 (April/May 2003), pp. 4 and 7–10; vote table, p. 34.

APFA presented the company's possible Section 1113 proposal as a more costly alternative to the RPA. Its explanation said American would seek $470 million in annual flight-attendant savings in bankruptcy, compared with $340 million outside bankruptcy, and warned that rejection proceedings could move quickly. The same document listed major proposed work-rule changes, including 50 percent deadhead, vacation reductions, reserve changes, sick-pay reductions, crew-meal elimination, per diem cuts, benefit elimination for overage leaves, and a proposed DB-plan freeze under the company-standard benefits proposal. APFA 1113 explanation. Section 1113 ordinarily required a hearing within 14 days after the application was filed and a ruling within 30 days after the hearing began, subject to the statute's extension provisions. 11 U.S.C. § 1113(d), 2002 edition.

American's 2011–2012 bankruptcy agreement

AMR filed Chapter 11 on November 29, 2011. American and APFA entered that process with the 2001 agreement as modified by the 2003 RPA. APFA's Foundation Document later compiled the 2001 agreement as modified by the RPA and related language; the Conditional Labor Agreement then served as the merger bridge into New American bargaining.

American's 2011–2012 bankruptcy produced further concessions after the 2003 RPA. The last, best and final offer (LBFO) process and resulting agreement established the American terms carried into joint bargaining, subject to the Conditional Labor Agreement and later merger protocols.

The American flight-attendant plan was frozen effective November 1, 2012, with benefits for existing participants preserved under its terms. The US Airways East flight-attendant plan had been terminated in 2005 and transferred to PBGC; eligible participants could still receive PBGC-administered benefits. Former America West employees had a separate defined-contribution retirement history. The later American/APFA CBA retains separate legacy retirement letters, but their coexistence is not proof that every employee within either legacy group had identical benefits. Letter 7; PBGC plan record; America West plan filing.

The United/Continental retirement comparison examines a parallel legacy disparity under a common employer, while distinguishing continued accrual under the Continental Retirement Plan (CARP) from American's frozen pension obligations. It also traces the later United agreement's LOA 4 discussion of a cost-neutral defined-benefit plan, which does not itself require implementation or restore historical losses.

Representation and transition architecture

The American merger separated four decisions: the contract applicable during transition, participation in joint bargaining, the identity of the certified representative, and responsibility for unfinished legacy-contract work. They were addressed by different instruments rather than resolved automatically at the corporate closing. The signed April 12, 2012 Conditional Labor Agreement (CLA) anticipated an expedited route to a joint contract; the later ABR and four-party NPA established CWA-AFA's role alongside APFA.

NMB single-carrier / representation sequence

Under the NMB merger process, the key question was whether American and US Airways had become a single transportation system for the flight-attendant craft or class. After the NMB single-carrier finding, potential intervenors had a limited window to challenge, and under the rule applied in this proceeding a challenger needed a 50 percent showing of interest. No rival intervenor filed. The NMB then extended APFA certification to the combined American / US Airways flight-attendant craft or class.

CWA-AFA did not lose a conventional public election against APFA at New American. CWA-AFA had already negotiated the transition architecture through the ABR, while APFA then became the certified representative through the NMB's merger procedures.

The parties' joint stipulation, paragraphs 4–5 places the single-transportation-system finding on July 29, 2014 and the extension of APFA certification on September 2, 2014. These are separate steps from the December 2013 corporate closing and the December 2014 JCBA award.

APFA / CWA-AFA maintenance, dues, and grievance administration

The Maintenance Agreement, signed July 29 and July 31, 2014 assigned continued legacy-contract work to CWA-AFA after certification transferred. Its initial period ran through JCBA effectiveness plus 90 days, with agreed extensions. APFA would remit 100 percent of LUS dues for covered employment months. A later grievance-only period could not extend beyond two years after JCBA effectiveness; its payment percentage depended on a separately agreed schedule. The agreement itself supplies no final declining-percentage table.

Covered services included investigations, grievances, arbitration, scheduling, reserve, safety, benefits, and other contract matters. Unrelated government relations, communications, and ordinary governance were excluded. The agreement also allocated expenses and indemnification responsibilities. These are service and liability provisions, not proof of the actual amount paid. Maintenance Agreement, paragraphs 1–4 and 9–10.

In its announcement dated April 1, 2015, APFA reported that it had been forwarding all LUS dues and was taking over the described representation work. CWA-AFA would continue outstanding legacy grievances, supported by a portion of dues. This contemporaneous account documents the handoff, but does not provide an audited payment total or the agreed percentage schedule.

A separate employer-funding arrangement authorized up to $3 million combined for specified joint-negotiation, arbitration, and seniority-integration expenses. It excluded claims against the company and efforts to influence representation choices. That cap was not the amount of APFA's dues-based servicing payments to CWA-AFA. Appendix D, NPA C.1–2.

Why United negotiated flying limits

On October 31, 2002, a United flight attendant asked CWA-AFA's predecessor organization, then known as AFA, why its recovery proposal offered only five extra hours through RDO, the process for transferring flying between attendants. The member wanted more work to offset a wage loss. The Financial Review Committee acknowledged that interest and said that preserving jobs across the membership took priority. It estimated that adding five hours to the monthly scheduled maximum would cost about 1,400 flight attendant jobs. It expected an RDO-only increase to have little effect on employment and identified a possible effect on reserves. The MEC directed further study of lifting the limits.

That contemporaneous explanation establishes an intentional bargaining choice. The 1,400 figure was AFA’s forecast for United’s operation at the time. It was neither a count of layoffs nor a measure of how many people the airline hired. The committee spoke before AFA affiliated with CWA. Its explanation identifies protecting employment and seniority progression. A dues-revenue purpose would require further evidence.

What the federal rule measured

In 2013, 14 CFR §121.467 ordinarily limited a scheduled duty period to 14 hours, with longer periods under specified crew-augmentation conditions. Career earnings and Social Security

Suppose an attendant willing and legally able to fly loses ten additional paid hours every month at $35 per hour, with no replacement trip or offsetting guarantee payment. That is $350 a month, $4,200 a year, or $84,000 in gross pay over 20 years at that constant rate. This scenario illustrates the scale of an effective constraint. Establishing an actual loss requires trip availability, award order, the employee’s bid and legalities, and the resulting pay record.

For a separate Social Security illustration, assume the same $84,000 is expressed in SSA wage-indexed dollars, falls below each year’s taxable maximum, and all of it changes the worker’s highest 35 years. Dividing by 420 raises average indexed monthly earnings by $200. If all $200 falls in the 32% bracket, the full-retirement-age monthly benefit differs by about $64 before rounding or subsequent adjustments. The effect can be zero for earnings already above the taxable maximum or years that never enter the top 35. The conversion from nominal pay to indexed earnings depends on the years involved.

Deadhead, training, vacation, and sick leave

The merger combined different methods of valuing time. A paid hour could count differently toward a scheduling limit, a training payment could replace protection for a missed trip, and vacation earned in one year could remain subject to older rules when taken the next year. These differences help explain why an increase in a published rate did not produce the same result for every flight attendant.

Deadhead pay and scheduling credit

At merger entry, American paid required deadheads to cover or return from flying assignments at 100 percent of scheduled flight time, but counted 50 percent toward flight-time limitations. Thus, a four-hour scheduled deadhead produced four paid hours and two hours against those limits. American Article 8.G.1, PDF p. 3. APFA's December 17, 2013 compensation summary confirms this distinction. Contemporary compensation guidance.

The US Airways Red Book provided full pay and credit using scheduled block time or actual flight time, whichever was greater. Its implementation letter brought the 100-percent provision forward to the first bid month after signing. Red Book §16.A, printed p. 16-1 and Letter 2, printed p. L2-6.

Union decisions and merger bargaining

The documents support evaluating decisions and outcomes separately. A union's organizational form does not, by itself, establish why a contract was delayed, why members rejected it, or why a pension was frozen rather than terminated. The bankruptcy histories discussed above established different inherited benefits; the merger agreements then allocated bargaining authority, representation, and implementation responsibilities.

In the America West / US Airways process, CWA-AFA's February 2011 account identified separate East and West MEC presidents and negotiations underway since January 2006. Common affiliation therefore coexisted with distinct legacy leadership and an unresolved joint contract. Its accusations about management's motives are the union's account, not an independently established explanation of every year of delay. The September 2012 rejection announcement reported 51 percent voting against the TA, 85 percent participation, and continued NMB oversight. The prolonged negotiations involved both common union affiliation and distinct legacy leadership, followed by a membership rejection.

APFA's advance process planning is documented in the signed April 2012 CLA and December 2012 clarification. The four-party NPA later established a different timetable and arbitration standard, while the ABR gave CWA-AFA equal negotiating-committee representation and protected consideration of the LUS agreement. The American result was consequently not APFA acting alone; both unions participated in designing the later process. ABR A–B.

After the combined American and US Airways membership rejected the tentative agreement, the previously agreed arbitration process produced a binding award. The protocol also limited the arbitrators' economic authority. They applied that limit in denying APFA's requested benefit options and wage retroactivity. APFA obtained management's agreement to restore the TA wage table on December 18. The December 23 wage agreements subsequently set the general January rates and a separate rate for the listed sixth-year LUS employees. Opinion, pages 12–19; December 18 wage-restoration announcement.

A faster joint-contract result does not establish faster implementation of every protection, nor does the record prove that CWA-AFA could have obtained the same employer commitment during the earlier merger. At American, rejection activated a previously agreed arbitration process. The resulting contract and its later implementation had different effects on wages, scheduling, and inherited benefits.

Bridge from the 2014 JCBA to the 2024–2029 APFA / American CBA

The JCBA's wage schedule changed through post-award agreements and later amendments. For example, APFA's May 1, 2017 announcement records Executive Committee approval of additional raises ranging from 4.2 to 6.5 percent by seniority, in addition to scheduled adjustments. The 2019 compilation includes these amendments and provides the wage baseline for the 2024 successor agreement.

The 2024–2029 CBA is the later successor agreement examined here. Its Letter 10 provides a one-time ratification bonus calculated from eligible earnings in 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, and January–August 2024. That lookback does not make the contract a separate 2020–2024 agreement or make all 2024 work rules retroactive to 2020. Long-term contract comparison. The successor APFA/American CBA records later wage and work-rule changes after the merger-era JCBA. Those changes coexist with continuing distinctions among eligible legacy retirement-plan participants; preserving those distinctions is not itself a new systemwide pension gain.

Selected chronology

  • November 1998: APFA/American contract became amendable; negotiations later proceeded through mediation and strike-preparation phases.
  • September 12, 2001: APFA members ratified the 2001 agreement by a 96 percent vote, one day after the September 11 attacks.
  • 2001–2002: American implemented the new agreement while facing September 11, Flight 587, TWA-LLC integration, furloughs, overage leaves, and implementation grievances.
  • February–April 2003: American demanded major labor-cost reductions; APFA negotiated the RPA under a threatened bankruptcy deadline and member ratification pressure.
  • May–July 2003: RPA implementation began with May 1 pay-rate, medical-contribution and part-time changes. Trip-selection objectives followed in the June contractual month and revised layover-rest rules in July. Vacation changes used separate rebid and accrual dates.
  • January 10 / February 1, 2005: US Airways flight-attendant DB pension terminated and PBGC trusteeship began.
  • November 29, 2011: AMR filed Chapter 11.
  • 2012: APFA moved through the LBFO / New CBA process during American's Chapter 11 restructuring; legacy American DB pension was frozen rather than terminated.
  • February 28, 2013: US Airways flight attendants ratified the combined US Airways / CWA-AFA Red Book.
  • December 9, 2013: American / US Airways merger closed.
  • 2014: NMB certification sequence extended APFA representation to the combined craft or class; the JCBA TA was rejected and the NPA arbitration backstop produced the final JCBA path.
  • November 9, 2014: Combined members rejected the tentative agreement by 16 votes; the agreed arbitration process followed. Appendix A, paragraph 8.
  • December 13, 2014: The award adopted the TA except for Section 3.A, substituted Appendix E, denied the two requested benefit-parity options, and made the JCBA effective. Award.
  • December 18, 2014: APFA announced management's agreement to restore the TA wage table effective January 1, 2015. Announcement.
  • December 23, 2014. The revised general wage agreement set the January 2015 top step at $55.58. A separate letter specified $39.34 for the listed LUS sixth-year employees. General wage agreement · LUS sixth-year letter.
  • April 1, 2015: APFA announced its assumption of the described LUS representation work, with outstanding legacy grievances remaining under the maintenance arrangement. Announcement.
  • September 12, 2024. Members ratified the successor agreement with 87 percent voting in favor and 95 percent participation, according to APFA. Its new wage scale began October 1. The agreement included a one-time bonus calculated from eligible earnings during 2020–2023 and January–August 2024. Ratification announcement and wage schedule.

Glossary

  • ABR: Agreement on Bargaining and Representation between CWA-AFA and APFA.
  • APFA: Association of Professional Flight Attendants, legacy American and later New American representative.
  • CBA: Collective bargaining agreement.
  • CWA-AFA: Association of Flight Attendants, affiliated with the Communications Workers of America since 2004.
  • DB: Defined benefit, a pension structure promising benefits under a specified formula.
  • FAA: Federal Aviation Administration.
  • JCBA: Joint Collective Bargaining Agreement.
  • LAA: Legacy American Airlines flight-attendant group.
  • LOA: Letter of agreement.
  • LUS: Legacy US Airways flight attendants, including the former US Airways East and America West groups.
  • MTA: Merger Transition Agreement.
  • NMB: National Mediation Board.
  • NPA: Negotiations Protocol Agreement.
  • PBS: Preferential Bidding System.
  • RPA: APFA / American Restructuring Participation Agreement negotiated in 2003 to avoid Chapter 11.
  • Section 1113: Bankruptcy Code process allowing a debtor to seek rejection or modification of a collective bargaining agreement, subject to statutory requirements and court approval.
  • SSA: Social Security Administration.
  • TA: Tentative agreement.
