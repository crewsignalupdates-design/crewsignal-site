What this history establishes

Continental/United offers a documented test of competing representation strategies. CWA-AFA and IAM sought the same combined bargaining unit, members chose CWA-AFA, and separate decisions followed about interim contracts, joint bargaining, retirement protection and voluntary flying. Continental Micronesia (CMI) remained a distinct legacy group within that process.

The evidence supports specific criticism and specific qualifications. Senior United base rates, lost future pension accrual and earlier voluntary-flying restrictions gave substance to IAM's stated priorities. The record also contains United pay advantages at some service steps, AFA pension-preservation efforts and later CWA-AFA improvements. Representation choice, contractual entitlement and economic outcome require separate tests.

The full comparator supplies the detailed contract, pension and implementation matrices. This companion examines the decisions behind those outcomes, including the earlier 1987–1991 bargaining interval and the rejected 2015 procedure.

1987–1991: a rejected agreement, a conditional buyout and a bargaining pause

Nearly four years separated the predecessor's November 1987 amendability from ratification of an ordinary successor in September 1991. The interval included a rejected proposal, a conditional employee-buyout agreement and suspension of normal negotiations. Those events matter when assessing responsibility for the delay. The union was known as AFA in these contemporary records. BLS settlement account, printed p.57; NMB annual report, printed p.11.

The eventual settlement included a reported $23 million retroactive-pay pool, initial increases of 6–9% from September 1 and later 4% increases. Any assessment of the cost of waiting must include those payments and improvements. Their existence establishes neither full compensation for the interval nor an individual employee's loss. There were other bargaining mechanisms at the same employer. BLS records a November 1989 United pilot interim agreement and an IAM ground-workforce settlement with a 4% increase retroactive to November 1989. The pilot interim pay terms are not established here, and another craft's terms were not necessarily obtainable by flight attendants. The comparison nevertheless makes the accountability question concrete: what interim relief or backdating did AFA seek, what did United offer or refuse, and why? BLS, printed pp.28–29.

The complete agreement governing 1986, its amendments and original flying limits remain unavailable in the examined record. Later reproductions of 1986 sideletters and archival catalog entries identify useful records but do not authenticate the missing pay and hours clauses. A judgment about avoidable delay requires the original proposals, member instructions, mediation decisions and buyout-pause terms. Reproduced 1986 Scope letter; Cornell agreement holding; Cornell 1986–1987 material.

Why was a joint-representation alternative not implemented?

The unions exchanged a concrete cooperation proposal and response before the representation contest. That exchange establishes a difference over negotiating strategy, while leaving a formal association or protected handoff unproved. CWA-AFA represented United; IAM represented Continental and CMI. NMB included all three in its April 1, 2011 single-system determination and certified CWA-AFA for the combined craft or class after the election. July 23 proposal; August 20 response; Single-system determination; June 30 certification.

Cooperation could have taken more than one form: an association of incumbent unions; a temporary bargaining arrangement preserving their respective rights; or a negotiated handoff to one representative with protected participation and servicing. Those are different from separate legacy administration or a multi-legacy committee inside one certified union. An earlier NMB decision records an actual CWA/IBT association for US Airways/America West passenger-service employees. It demonstrates a precedent, not an offered flight-attendant solution at CO/UA. 33 NMB No. 27, association and member-consent record.

Management's May 24, 2010 communication contemplated joint bargaining as an alternative to continuing standalone Section 6 talks. The later NMB decision records management's account that only ALPA accepted the joint route at that stage. The general invitation is relevant evidence, but it is not a detailed CWA-AFA/IAM governance proposal or a ruling that one union caused the subsequent delay. Company communication; 38 NMB No. 38, printed p.129.

On July 23, 2010, CWA-AFA proposed coordination of the unions' separate Section 6 negotiations, information sharing, common negotiating positions and exploration of a global solution. IAM's August 20 reply said its bargaining course reflected member priorities and could not be changed at that stage without membership approval. It also endorsed continued cooperation. Its priorities included better pay steps, pension protection and voluntary flying flexibility. Cooperation proposal, pp.1–2; IAM reply, pp.1–2.

The reply should be evaluated as a particular strategic choice. It does not establish that IAM permanently rejected every form of cooperation, that either side accepted an enforceable multiunion protocol, or that a completed representation-sharing agreement was available for signature.

Both unions articulated legacy-contract priorities. In the June 2010 hearing record, CWA-AFA urged completion of United's existing talks before closing; IAM explained its concern about prematurely combining employees. IAM's February 25, 2011 ratification announcement described its Continental interim settlement and anticipated later negotiations after winning combined representation. These sources require examining both organizations' strategies, not assigning Continental's pre-certification decisions to CWA-AFA. Senate hearing, printed pp.22 and 75–79; IAM announcement.

What employees were choosing in 2011

IAM promoted its Continental interim settlement and the gains it would seek if elected. CWA-AFA's May 19 campaign notice paired a commitment to pursue the best existing provisions with an explicit explanation that the representation election would not itself replace the separate contracts. Both parts belong in the account: a bargaining program could be tested against later choices, but it was not an employer commitment to grant the combined package. IAM settlement announcement; CWA campaign notice.

The June 29 tally gave CWA-AFA 11,942 votes and IAM 9,745. The 21,780 valid votes also included 28 for other choices and 65 for no representation; five void ballots were recorded separately. Certification followed June 30. The approximately 15,147 UA and 9,458 CO/CMI employees described at the election-authorization stage do not reveal how members of either group voted in the secret ballot. Election authorization, printed p.183; Official tally, printed p.248.

The NMB disposition included both a surviving result and corrective action

In its May 30, 2012 decision, NMB found no carrier interference and no basis to overturn the election. It found no evidence that CWA-AFA actually tracked votes through hyperlinks. Continued voting links and voting at the Los Angeles event nevertheless raised serious confidentiality concerns; voting at the Chicago event was not established. NMB shortened the ordinary bar on another representation application to 18 months after certification and announced voting-process changes. Certification survived; the shortened application bar did not make it expire. NMB findings and disposition, printed pp.395–402.

The decision therefore supports scrutiny of the voting environment while preserving the result. Neither the numerical outcome nor certification authenticates every campaign claim. Allegations, agency findings and the remedy must be distinguished.

Testing the priorities: pay, pension protection and voluntary flying

IAM's stated priorities corresponded to material differences in the agreements. Testing those differences requires matching service, dates, paid credit and employee eligibility. The contracts support specific conclusions more readily than a universal ranking of either union. August 2010 priorities.

Pay: a senior-rate disadvantage, with meaningful exceptions

For April–June 2012, a controlled example at the matched 14th-year domestic step and 75 paid hours produces $3,279.92 under UA's monthly-salary formula and $3,633.75 under CO's rate, a $353.83 CO advantage. At the matched seventh-year step, the same method gives UA a $62.06 advantage. These calculations exclude extra credits and premiums and assume no CO quarterly incentive. They are contract illustrations, not employee payroll or annual-loss estimates. UA Section 5.A.1; CO Section 4.A; full pay and credit comparison.

The December 3, 1997 United arbitration letter expressly included work-rule costs in the compensation mechanism. That supports examination of the tradeoff between rules and cash pay. It supplies no separate price for a flying-hours ceiling. Credit guarantees could also produce higher pay for some work profiles, and the 2016 JCBA established a common initial $62 top base rate. 1997 letter, A.2–A.3 and B.2; 2016 pay table, Section 4.A.

Pensions: unequal outcomes and documented safeguards

United's flight-attendant plan terminated June 30, 2005, ending new accrual under that plan. PBGC-administered benefits remained subject to applicable limits; the termination did not eliminate every pension payment. The court record documents AFA's efforts to preserve the plan and upheld termination on grounds that do not establish union inaction or prove what another union could have secured. PBGC plan record; District-court opinion, pp.2–5 and 33.

Continental's 2006 pension letter provided a member choice and an employer-contribution fallback if CARP were frozen or terminated, subject to its terms. The 2016 JCBA retained CARP for covered legacy participants and a related fallback, while improving direct employer contributions for legacy UA. It did not restore UA's lost defined-benefit accrual. Preservation, fallback protection and restoration are different outcomes. 2006 LOA 27; 2016 Section 29.I and LOA 6; retirement comparison.

Voluntary flying: a real historical restriction and a material change in 2012

The recovered 2001 and later pre-May-2012 United provisions limited voluntary credit-hour projections in ways Continental's provisions did not impose on regular full-time employees. Continental nevertheless retained operational conditions and a 55-hour limit for specified jobshare, half-month-leave and partnership arrangements. Incentive-pay ceilings were separate from permission to pick up work. United archived Section 9; Continental Section 5.G.5.

United moved to the new monthly system in May 2012. Its education guide explained voluntary opt-up above 100, protection against forced opt-up and a job-distribution rationale for the default maximum. This permits a direct examination of the tradeoff between sharing work and individual earning opportunity. It also prevents treating the post-May default as a continuing absolute prohibition on more flying. April 24 implementation notice; Monthly-maximum explanation.

Where an employee wanted additional work, had a legal trip available and was blocked by a contractual ceiling, the rule constrained earning opportunity. Measuring the loss requires evidence of those conditions, the applicable credit treatment and the attainable trips. The existing record does not quantify cap-attributable hiring or establish a dues-driven motive. Detailed hours, earnings and staffing analysis.

What the FACC archive contributes

The Flight Attendant Coalition for Change archive asked substantive questions about contract economics, signing bonuses, work rules and the information available to members. Its value lies in the specific claims and clauses that can be checked. Advocacy should be attributed and assessed under the same standard applied to CWA-AFA, IAM and management communications. January 2012 clause-based critique; July 2012 education and survey notices.

Its August 15, 2012 article comparing July pay uses the same flying and deadhead inputs for both contracts. The displayed totals differ by $1,114.40, including $66.65 of per diem. The example therefore examines compensation for common work; it does not measure earnings from flying additional hours. Continental Section 9.F normally limits regular sick-leave withdrawals to restoring month-end pay to 93 hours, unless more than 500 hours remain in the bank after withdrawal. If the line represents regular sick leave, the illustration requires that exception; the retained inputs do not establish the bank balance or an alternative qualifying basis. Original pay illustration; Continental Section 9.F.

A separate comparison mixes four- and six-year allocations of a $5,000 bonus. Spread over six years, that amount is $69.44 per month; over four years, it is $104.17. A consistent period is necessary for a meaningful comparison. Survey safeguards against repeat submissions were disclosed, but they do not establish a representative sample. These qualifications preserve the archive's useful questions while preventing unverified payroll, survey or cumulative-loss claims from becoming findings. A 2012 article also cannot establish what voters received before the 2011 election. Bonus comparison; Disclosed survey controls.

Institutional interests are part of the history. At the July 2011 CWA convention, Veda Shook described United as half of CWA-AFA's membership and acknowledged the stakes of losing it. That documents organizational interests; it does not establish that those interests caused a particular cap, concession or crossover decision. Such a conclusion requires evidence connecting the institutional concern to the specific decision. Convention proceedings, printed p.115.

Separate settlements before joint bargaining: a strategic choice

After certification, United and CWA-AFA agreed to expedite a legacy-United settlement before joint negotiations. The February 2012 union announcement reported an immediate 10 percent wage increase and a $5,000 signing bonus and expressly described improvements before moving to a single contract. Interim gains therefore cannot be dismissed as imaginary, nor does their existence demonstrate that the chosen sequence was optimal. September 2011 company announcement; February 2012 union announcement.

Section 35.B of the 2012 United agreement required notice 30–60 days after ratification and joint negotiations within 120 days after notice. This was a starting timetable, not a deadline for finishing the JCBA. The union announced the joint launch on August 27, 2012, after separate subsidiary agreements. The full comparator distinguishes that launch from the first substantive sessions in December. 2012 agreement, Section 35.B; August 27 announcement; joint-bargaining chronology.

Assessment. Placing separate settlements ahead of joint bargaining contributed to its timing and should be evaluated as a strategy, not described solely as an external constraint. The unresolved counterfactual is whether a feasible parallel or earlier joint process would have delivered a better overall result sooner. That judgment requires considering interim earnings, concessions, preparation, representation authority and the terms management was willing to accept.

A stronger starting agreement is not a guarantee of additive improvements in a successor. Management can price both stages together. The appropriate test is what the first stage purchased and what opportunity it consumed: immediate relief, protection of an existing process, narrower remaining disputes, or a dependable next stage. A commitment to commence talks does not itself supply the last of those.

Members ratified the joint agreement on August 12, 2016; United reported full flight-attendant JCBA implementation in October 2018. Those are separate milestones. The elapsed time is an outcome members can evaluate, but assigning all of it to one representative requires dated evidence about the proposals, employer responses, member votes, mediation and implementation work. 2016 ratification; October 2018 implementation report.

The August 2015 proposal and rejection

United proposed a further negotiated timetable followed, if necessary, by a final-offer recommendation. CWA-AFA leadership rejected that format. The union's account dates discussions to August 17; the written company proposal is dated August 18. The documents concern a specific voluntary process for the three-group JCBA, not a formal NMB proffer or a seniority or grievance case. A nonbinding proposal still merits serious evaluation

The rejection was consequential as a choice even though the process did not guarantee a contract. A scheduled neutral recommendation might narrow differences or produce an acceptable package. It might instead influence later bargaining unfavorably, consume time or fail either approval process. These are competing possibilities, not findings about an event that never occurred.

The union's explanation acknowledged that the recommendation would be nonbinding. It feared that a detrimental recommendation would nevertheless become a basis for later bargaining. Thus the question was not merely preservation of a member vote—the proposal retained one—but the merits and risks of its selection method and standards. Stated reasons for rejection.

Leadership's judgment should be examined against the actual text, its description to members and any counterproposals. Management's choices also require scrutiny: retaining its own approval, setting economic and selection criteria, and responding to possible modifications. The record reviewed here does not establish a revised executed process or a counterproposal that resolved those objections. It therefore supports evaluating the decision, not computing a definite number of months it caused.

Why the American arbitration mechanism was different

The American NPA required final and binding interest arbitration if a tentative JCBA was not reached or was rejected, within its agreed economic and effectiveness provisions. United's proposal retained post-recommendation approval. An enforceable decision on unresolved terms and an approval-conditioned recommendation are different bargaining mechanisms. NPA A.5 and B.

The comparison concerns process, employee choice, substantive value and implementation separately. Faster resolution does not establish better terms for every employee; retaining a vote does not establish that an open-ended alternative is costless. The American award and implementation analysis follows the consequences of that binding mechanism.

What other merger transitions can—and cannot—show

CO/UA involved competing incumbent unions and a head-to-head representation election. AirTran/Southwest and US Airways/American used negotiated transitions with employee approval of agreements. Those approvals should not be erased by saying there was “no vote,” or recast as contested NMB union-choice ballots.

These cases supply mechanisms to examine in any future contest or merger: what members approve, how legacy groups participate, what contract services continue, and which process resolves stalled bargaining. They cannot establish that TWU, APFA, IAM or CWA-AFA would reproduce another carrier's result. A challenge at an already represented carrier also differs from a merger representation proceeding; historical application bars and vote procedures should not be assumed to govern a future case.

What the record establishes—and what remains open

The comparison supports accountability for identifiable choices. The 2010 letters document competing strategies; the 2011 election selected a representative; later contracts document different pay, pension and flying outcomes. The 1987–1991 history shows that a conditional buyout and a bargaining pause must be included in any assessment of delay. The 2015 documents establish rejection of a particular approval-conditioned process, with its potential benefits and risks.

The remaining records are specific: the original 1986 agreement and amendments; the 1987 proposal and ballot; the 1990 conditional agreement and pause terms; interim-relief proposals; work-rule cost calculations; pension alternatives and later study records; and the original financial communications and participation evidence needed to test crossover decisions. Their absence limits the allocation of responsibility and the measurement of economic harm. It does not erase the documented outcomes or establish that an alternative union would necessarily have produced a better result.

The America West/US Airways companion supplies the earlier bargaining-track history. Across the cases, the relevant tests are the benefits of each stage, its opportunity costs, the information given to members and the means available to reach a completed agreement.
