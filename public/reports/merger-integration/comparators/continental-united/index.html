United and Continental combined their businesses in October 2010, but their flight attendants did not begin working under a joint collective bargaining agreement (JCBA) until 2016. Common scheduling followed in October 2018. Across those years, three predecessor groups—United, Continental, and Continental Micronesia—continued to carry different pay structures, work rules, and retirement arrangements into the combined airline. [1] [2] [3]

The differences were larger than an hourly-pay comparison could show. Continental’s senior rates exceeded United’s, while United’s credit guarantees could produce more paid time for some trips. Reserve guarantees bought different availability obligations. Medical coverage depended on retirement dates and plan elections. Retirement benefits reflected both what had survived earlier bankruptcies and what could still be earned afterward. [4] [5] [6] [7]

United’s flight-attendant pension terminated in 2005 and entered Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation administration. Continental’s covered nonpilot employees continued accruing benefits under the Continental Airlines Retirement Plan, or CARP. Micronesia had its own retirement history and later IAM National Pension Plan participation. The 2016 agreement improved common compensation but preserved important distinctions between those retirement groups. [8] [9] [7] [2]

The comparable US Airways/American retirement history presents a parallel, not an identical, disparity. US Airways flight attendants’ plan entered PBGC trusteeship; American’s defined-benefit plans were frozen rather than terminated. A frozen sponsor-maintained pension, continued CARP accrual, and benefits payable from a terminated PBGC-trusteed plan are different outcomes. A common employer and a common labor agreement did not erase the financial consequences of those differences. [10] [11] [9]

1. Which agreements belong in the comparison?

Three dates define the comparison: the October 1, 2010 corporate closing; the subsequent subsidiary agreements negotiated in 2011–2012; and the 2016 joint agreement and its phased implementation. The date printed in a later contract’s title is not necessarily the date on which its promises first became binding. Continental’s agreement labeled 2010–2012, for example, was ratified in February 2011 with retroactive terms. It cannot simply replace the earlier agreement as the account of what employees were promised at closing. [1] [12] [13]

2. Different bankruptcy histories before the merger

Continental: UFA in 1983, IAM in the later rebuilding

Continental’s first bankruptcy was a Union of Flight Attendants history, not an IAM flight-attendant negotiation. In 1983 Continental halted domestic operations, filed Chapter 11, sought rejection of its ALPA and UFA labor contracts, and resumed domestic flying under Emergency Work Rules. ALPA and UFA struck. The ensuing litigation concerned the pre-§ 1113 legal framework: Congress enacted § 1113 in July 1984, after this bankruptcy petition. [17] [18]

The Fifth Circuit’s 1990 decision did not restore the rejected agreements or award every claimant all projected future wages. It allowed a limited unsecured-damages inquiry into the period when work would have remained available without rejection, while upholding the denial of rejection damages for strike periods. Those proceedings arose from the UFA-era rejection, rather than an IAM-negotiated bankruptcy settlement. [17]

The representation transition was itself contested. On October 31, 1990, the National Mediation Board transferred Continental flight-attendant certification from UFA to IAM after investigating the unions’ merger. Continental sought a new election and conducted a post-transfer poll. A federal district court enjoined the poll as interference with IAM’s certification. The related litigation describes the transfer request and the Board’s conclusion that UFA had merged into IAM. [19] [20]

Continental filed its second Chapter 11 case on December 3, 1990. Its 1995 Form 10-K dates confirmation of the reorganization plan to April 16, 1993 and effectiveness to April 27. The company attributed the filing principally to recessionary revenue declines, extreme price competition, and fuel-price increases associated with the Persian Gulf War. Contemporary reporting added the pressures of debt and cash shortages. By the 1997 annual report, flight-attendant wage and per-diem improvements formed part of the post-bankruptcy rebuilding record. [21] [22] [23]

For the corresponding East-side restructuring sequence, see the America West/US Airways bankruptcy and contract history. The different starting agreements are also relevant to the later comparison of bargaining timelines in Section 13.

United: the long-duration agreement and two restructuring rounds

United’s 1997 annual report described a new ten-year flight-attendant agreement. The contractual letters use the formulation “1996–2001 and 2001–2006 Agreement.” The company’s description emphasized the overall duration; the contractual letters identified the two successive periods. The 2012 book still incorporated letters dated October 2, 1997, along with later bankruptcy-era commitments. [24] [14]

United entered Chapter 11 in December 2002. Flight attendants ratified the first restructuring agreement on April 29, 2003; the labor changes took effect May 1. A further agreement effective January 7, 2005 replaced the 2003–2009 agreement. Pension termination later in 2005 was a separate event, not simply another name for either wage settlement. [25] [26] [8]

3. What United’s concessions changed—and what the $300 million measures

United’s 2003 Form 10-K estimated that the flight-attendant restructuring would reduce average annual costs by approximately $300 million compared with the previous agreement. The same filing separately described approximately $300 million in salaried and management savings. Those are distinct figures for different groups. The broader company labor program combined pay, productivity, medical-benefit, and staffing changes; its company-wide savings cannot be assigned wholly to flight attendants. [25]

Contemporary Associated Press reporting described a 9% flight-attendant wage reduction, work-rule changes, and fewer holidays, reporting the package at $314 million annually, or approximately $1.9 billion over six years. Neither provides an itemized reconciliation assigning a specified share of the total to each wage, scheduling, medical, or pension change. [27] [25]

The concrete mechanisms are nevertheless visible. A lower wage rate reduced pay for the same credited work; changed credit and scheduling rules could alter how much paid time a trip generated; reduced holiday coverage affected premium opportunities; and medical and pension changes shifted costs or reduced future benefits. For a hypothetical $40 rate, a 9% cut yields $36.40, or $270 less for 75 identically paid hours. This is a stated-assumption example, not a wage-table entry. That arithmetic illustrates the wage mechanism; it does not explain the entire $300 million forecast. [27] [25]

The restructuring summaries explain negotiated changes; §§ 5–8 of the contract govern compensation and credits; § 33 and the pre-Medicare sideletter govern distinct medical cohorts; § 34 sets retirement terms. The later defined-contribution, distribution, and convertible-note materials address replacements or partial offsets, not continuation of the terminated pension. Their different purposes explain why a distribution payment or new savings contribution was not equivalent to restoring the earlier pension. [29] [6]

4. The 2003 retirement window and the later medical-benefit changes

More than 2,500 United flight attendants were reported to have retired before July 1, 2003 in response to an opportunity to preserve established retiree-medical terms. In a February 13, 2004 letter, members of Congress said some had accepted reduced early-retirement pensions to retain that medical protection. Their employment decisions were irreversible in a way that a later premium revision was not: they had left active employment, and some had committed to lower pension payments, before facing the subsequent medical changes. [30]

The cutoff had a contractual basis. A May 1, 2003 sideletter carried forward specified predecessor pre-Medicare contribution provisions for eligible flight attendants and expressly excluded those retiring on or after July 1, 2003. It preserved a service-based contribution schedule, a cost trigger tied to the December 31, 1991 base, and limits on increases—including a 20% limit during the participant’s retirement under that formula. The letter ran concurrently with the restructuring agreement. It was more than a general expectation of favorable insurance, but not an unconditional promise that all coverage and costs would remain unchanged for life. [29]

Later retirees faced a different arrangement. The 2003 book tied pre-Medicare contributions to service-related percentages of plan cost and priced post-Medicare coverage at plan cost less a $90 monthly company contribution per covered person. Eligibility exceptions, dependent coverage, and the actual election also mattered. A single active-employee premium percentage cannot describe every retiree’s coverage. [29]

The documentary counts describe different populations. The congressional letter referred to more than 2,500 pre-cutoff retirements. The bankruptcy court recited the union’s estimate of approximately 2,100 retirements during May and June. United’s 4,622 figure included all pre-cutoff flight-attendant retirees, including earlier retirees. The broader company total included people outside the retirement window; none of these counts establishes an equal financial loss for every person counted. [30] [31] [32]

Why the examiner mattered

The March 15, 2004 bankruptcy opinion recognized United’s reservation of the right to seek relief under § 1114, the bankruptcy provision governing specified retiree insurance benefits. It also addressed the dispute about when United decided to seek reductions. The union alleged that the decision predated the retirement cutoff; United denied that account. Appointment of an examiner authorized investigation. It was not itself a final finding of fraud, breach, or complete vindication. [31] [33]

United subsequently relied on the examiner’s account of the timing, while CWA-AFA continued to criticize nondisclosure. The party accounts do not establish a final finding of fraud or a complete exoneration. United’s reservation exposed the benefits to later modification; that exposure did not erase the consequences for employees who had retired to preserve the earlier terms. [32] [34]

The published 2004 agreement and its cost illustrations

The published June 9, 2004 Retiree Coalition agreement retained each covered participant’s existing base-contribution formula but added a surcharge for each covered adult, limited to two per family, with no dependent-child surcharge. Its initial flight-attendant schedule specified $22 monthly for a pre-65 adult and $16 for a post-65 adult. Enrollment changes were scheduled for September 1, 2004, or as soon thereafter as administratively practical. [35]

The consequence was not simply that every pre-cutoff retiree became identical to every later retiree. Some preserved base formulas continued, but surcharges and plan changes eroded the medical protection sought through retirement. This 2003–2004 history is separate from the 2005 pension termination. An employee could experience both without the two events having the same legal mechanism or financial effect. [35] [8]

5. How United’s pension was earned, reduced, and terminated

The earlier United agreement calculated post-1981 benefits using a percentage of participating earnings, with additional alternatives for older service. The 1997-version text used 2.22%. The 2003–2009 contract compilation protected a June 30, 2003 accrued-benefit floor calculated using 2.2669%, while introducing a new final-average-earnings formula effective July 1. Those percentages come from different document versions; the intervening amendment explaining the change in that percentage remains unidentified. [40] [29]

The 2003 benefit was the greater of the protected floor and 1.48% × final average eligible earnings × years of participation, capped at 35 years. Final average earnings used the highest consecutive sixty months within the last 120 months. Service, participation, vesting, and eligibility for an early-retirement subsidy were separate concepts. The two calculations were alternatives, not amounts to add together. [29]

For an illustration, assume a fully vested post-1981 entrant with twenty participation years on June 30, 2003, constant eligible annual and final-average earnings of $40,000, two further participation years, and a single-life annuity commencing at age sixty in June 2005. Assume no breaks, transferred-plan benefits, special payments, or other adjustments.

Calculation under those assumptions Annual benefit
Protected 2003 floor: 2.2669% × $40,000 × 20 $18,135.20
New formula at twenty years: 1.48% × $40,000 × 20 $11,840.00
New formula at twenty-two years: 1.48% × $40,000 × 22 $13,024.00

The floor prevents an immediate reduction in this example, but the extra two years do not yet increase the payable amount because the new calculation remains below it. The 2003 figure is an accrued benefit, not a pension payment assumed to start that year. This example neither values every employee’s loss nor determines the amount PBGC ultimately pays. [29]

The flight-attendant plan terminated and entered PBGC trusteeship effective June 30, 2005, case 19962800. PBGC lists 28,367 participants. Future benefit accrual under that terminated plan ceased, but termination did not eliminate every participant’s pension payment. [8]

PBGC’s historical maximum for a pension beginning at age sixty in 2005 was $2,470.74 monthly. Being below that ceiling does not alone guarantee full payment. Vesting, age and service eligibility, phase-in of recent increases, early-retirement supplements, survivor options, allocation of plan assets, and recoveries can affect the result. Some payments can exceed the guarantee through allocated assets. [41] [42] [43]

GAO’s contemporaneous estimates, based on January 1, 2005 PBGC data, anticipated cuts for 11,109 of 15,024 active United flight attendants, compared with 29 of 5,108 retired flight attendants. These were estimated pension impacts for those cohorts, not final individual awards, not all plan participants, and not measures of the retiree-medical changes. The difference illustrates why a universal statement that all retirees lost the same pension percentage is inaccurate. [43]

6. The effort to preserve United’s plan

CWA-AFA’s opposition included identifiable financing proposals. In June 22, 2005 congressional testimony, then-president Patricia Friend described a January 27 meeting with PBGC and a March 30 proposal to PBGC executive director Bradley Belt. Its ingredients included bankruptcy stock, redirected defined-contribution funding, a note comparable to one negotiated for pilots, and PBGC assistance through cash, a guarantee, a pension bond, or another mechanism. Friend attributed a favorable April 4 response to Belt and referred to analysis by PBGC expert Michael Kramer using United’s Gershwin 5.0 F projections. These are her documented accounts, not findings that the proposed financing was certain to succeed. [44]

United’s April 11 court memorandum described an April 5 proposal and April 8 counterproposal, both dependent on PBGC financing a shortfall. Management argued that preservation alternatives remained too costly and that exit lenders required the termination-based business plan and its downside protections. The filing cited actuarial and financial declarations by Timothy J. Marnell, Frank R. Colosi, and Elizabeth Hiszczynski. Those citations identify the support United offered; the pleading does not by itself establish that every conceivable alternative was impossible. [45]

The dispute therefore involved not only whether a pension should be preserved, but who would finance it and bear the risk. The proposals and responses demonstrate substantive negotiations; without the complete models, correspondence, and financing commitments, they do not establish the outcome that an unadopted package would have produced. [44] [45]

United and PBGC reached a settlement on April 22, 2005, approved by the bankruptcy court on May 10. The Seventh Circuit subsequently upheld that settlement approval while explaining that the settlement did not itself compel PBGC to terminate the flight-attendant plan. PBGC made a separate determination on June 23, effective June 30. The appellate decision did not decide the separate termination challenge. Contract rejection under § 1113 and pension termination under ERISA were separate legal steps. [46] [47] [18]

7. Underfunding did not lead to one inevitable outcome

GAO identified several interacting causes of the industry’s pension deterioration: investment losses, falling interest rates that increased measured liabilities, additional benefit obligations, and funding rules that permitted credit balances and asset smoothing to postpone cash contributions. Compliance with minimum funding requirements did not establish that assets were sufficient to pay benefits on termination. GAO’s specific United and US Airways credit-balance examples concerned pilot plans, not flight-attendant account-level findings. [43]

8. Continental’s pension choices—and Micronesia’s separate plan

Continental’s March 2005 tentative agreement would have frozen flight-attendant CARP accrual and substituted IAM National Pension Plan participation. Flight attendants rejected that agreement on March 30. Later in the year, the company reported continuing nonpilot accrual even though pilot accrual had frozen. A proposed freeze and an implemented freeze are different events. [54] [55] [9]

The executed February 1, 2006 Letter of Agreement (LOA) 27 gave mainline flight attendants a separate majority-vote choice between CARP and IAM NPP. Remaining in CARP preserved the plan without material change during the agreement’s term, including lump-sum rights; switching would freeze CARP accrual and initiate IAM-plan contributions while retaining accrued CARP benefits. The letter also prescribed IAM contributions if CARP were later frozen or terminated. IAM’s December 2005 announcement described a tentative agreement; the executed letter and subsequent pension-election history are separate evidence. [5] [13] [56] [57]

The 2010 CARP summary plan description gives conditional credit for service before September 1988, tied to specified employment, retirement, disability, leave, or transfer circumstances around April 27, 1993. It also describes later credit for pre-1984 service. These provisions establish retrospective recognition under the later plan; they do not establish that every predecessor benefit accrued continuously and unchanged through both bankruptcies, or that every former employee received every earlier year of service. [58]

Micronesia was not mainline CARP. Its June 2006 LOA 23 provided IAM NPP entry from January 1, 2007, with minimum contributions calculated as $1 × 1.891 × eligible paid hours, subject to plan limits. Contributions under the prior CMI retirement plan ceased while existing benefits were retained. The 2012 agreement incorporated that undertaking. IAM NPP participation is a multiemployer defined-benefit arrangement, not an individual 401(k) account. [7] [16]

A trip can reverse a rate-only comparison

Continental’s one-for-four trip rig applied only when a scheduled rest exceeded 29 hours. Micronesia’s closing-date agreement supplied a four-hour duty-period minimum rather than Continental’s conditional trip rig. Applicable guarantees are compared and offset, not piled on top of one another. [6] [5] [7]

11. Interim agreements, fences, and the crossover choice

Continental’s revised interim agreement was ratified February 25, 2011 by 68% of voting members, with 70% participation reported by IAM. It followed rejection of an earlier offer. The revised agreement provided retroactive pay, a higher wage scale reaching $52.53 at the top, a no-furlough guarantee for its duration, restored company 401(k) matching, and merger protections. The later aircraft-allocation fence took effect April 1, 2011 even though it used a May 2, 2010 fleet reference date. That reference date is not the letter’s effective date. [12] [13]

The earlier Continental agreement had already required successor assumption, fair-and-equitable seniority integration under specified provisions, separate inflight operations pending the designated integration steps, and protection against merger-caused furlough during that interval. Its expedited grievance-arbitration language enforced those protections. It did not authorize an arbitrator to impose a new joint contract. [5]

United employees ratified their separate 2012–2016 agreement on February 28, 2012 with 70.4% approval and 88.5% participation. Continental’s extension followed July 13 and Micronesia’s August 17. The latter’s union announcement reported “nearly 80 percent” approval, without establishing an exact numerator or turnout. These were subsidiary-contract votes, not votes on a combined JCBA. [70] [71] [72]

United’s 2012 agreement retained its bankruptcy-era structure while modifying it. A no-furlough letter referred to modifications of the 2005–2010 agreement, and § 34 retained the defined-contribution program effective January 1, 2006. By the February 2015 wage column, the book scheduled $46.86 at the top domestic step and $50.05 international. These later scheduled rates, rather than the initial 2012 column, are the relevant book-based comparison immediately before the JCBA, subject to any separately applicable amendments. [14]

The February 28, 2012 Cross Over Hiring letter offered a temporary, one-time election, opening the day after signing and closing thirty days later, with a one-time ten-day revocation opportunity. Transferring employees then worked under the subsidiary-Continental agreement structure. Prior United service counted for CARP vesting but not for CARP credited service; accrual began with the specified Continental employment/payroll transition. The option offered prospective access to a different arrangement, not restoration of all earlier United pension service. [14]

The distinction is central to the retirement comparison. An employee can be vested in a pension while having far fewer years included in its benefit calculation than in company seniority. A transfer that improves future coverage does not necessarily repair past losses. [14]

12. Bargaining choices on the way to the JCBA

The opening joint-bargaining phase was not simply a continuous sequence of substantive exchanges beginning at the August 2012 launch. In its December 7 report, the joint negotiating committee said it had offered November 20 and accepted United’s request for December 4–5. It reported bringing fifteen proposals but presenting three while discussions concentrated on procedural ground rules, meeting frequency, confidentiality, and bargaining method. These were the union’s descriptions of specific disagreements, not adjudicated findings of obstruction. [62] [73]

The signed June 25, 2014 protocol targeted July 23, 2015 for a complete tentative agreement, established steering and subject-matter processes, contemplated two consecutive bargaining weeks monthly where practical, and provided for NMB-assisted training and a progress review. It also addressed confidentiality, information exchange, and up to $2.5 million in reimbursement of negotiating expenses. Final company acceptance and union ratification remained necessary, and either party could terminate on thirty days’ notice. Missing the target did not automatically trigger binding arbitration. [63]

NMB facilitation in 2014 was distinct from formal mediation in 2015. The agency’s activity records identify facilitation docket F-0139 and, later, three mediation dockets covering United, Micronesia, and Continental. Application, docketing, notice, and the October 20 start were separate milestones. [74] [75] [65]

The August 2015 proposal and rejection

CWA-AFA’s August 19 account described an August 17 meeting involving MEC presidents Marcus Valentino, Ken Diaz, and Suzanne Hendricks and collective-bargaining director Joe Burns. The company’s written proposal is dated August 18, and its letters describe August 18 discussions. The meeting account and the dated written proposal therefore do not establish an identical presentation date for every term. [76] [77] [78]

United proposed renewed talks and, absent a complete agreement, a board that would consider comprehensive packages for unresolved matters. Existing tentative agreements would be preserved. The board generally would recommend one complete package unless both sides permitted issue-by-issue selection. The proposed timetable contemplated a recommendation by October 31 and subsequent acceptance decisions, with procedural dates subject to mutual adjustment. The signature lines were blank. [77]

The written proposal required verified compensation and cost comparisons with American and Delta applied to the combined United population. It did not expressly establish a numerical American/Delta average as a floor or ceiling. Its recommendation remained subject to each party’s internal acceptance process; statutory rights and thirty-day termination rights were retained. This was not an automatically binding award eliminating both membership approval and the company’s acceptance decision. [77]

Union leadership rejected the process, reporting advisers’ concerns about job security, profit sharing, and contract language and characterizing the economics as an American/Delta average. United’s August 21 response disputed that characterization and emphasized the retained approval rights. The proposal establishes its written mechanism; the competing statements establish the parties’ positions, not the complete content of their oral discussions. [76] [79] [77]

United separately gave notice terminating the 2014 protocol, alleging breaches involving information exchange, confidentiality, and public communications. CWA-AFA applied for formal mediation. The parties reached agreement on terms June 24, 2016, 311 days after the dated proposal, and members ratified it 360 days after the proposal. Neither interval proves that accepting the unchosen process would have delivered an earlier or better contract. No recommendation or award was issued under that proposal. [78] [64] [67] [69]

The published results list 20,923 eligible members,9,966 votes for,8,760 against,128 blank or no vote cast, and 18,854 total participation. The 9,966–8,760 split gives approximately 53.22% approval among yes/no votes, reported as 53%; the participation total yields 90.11% of eligible members. 13. America West/US Airways: common representation, prolonged separate contracts

America West and US Airways merged September 27, 2005. Both flight-attendant groups were represented by CWA-AFA, unlike the IAM/CWA-AFA representation contest at United/Continental. The absence of that interunion election did not itself produce a prompt common agreement. The transition arrangements and continued predecessor contracts formed the framework for a long bargaining period. [81] [82] [83]

Members rejected tentative agreements in March and September 2012. The union reported approximately 85% participation and 51% rejection in the September vote. A later strike authorization was an authorization, not evidence that a strike occurred or that NMB had released the parties. The common agreement was ratified February 28, 2013 with 80% approval reported—seven years, five months, one day after the merger closing. Implementation milestones followed separately. [84] [85] [86] [83] [87]

The comparison with United is therefore substantive: both workforces spent years under separate arrangements, but the representation histories, interim protections, disputed provisions, rejected agreements, and bargaining alternatives differed. Duration measures the period employees lived with those differences; it does not, on its own, allocate responsibility for every month. The America West/US Airways comparator examines the East/West agreements and successive proposals in greater detail.

Arbitration was not the same choice at each carrier

The documents cited here do not establish an early post-2005 America West/US Airways flight-attendant interest-arbitration proposal and rejection. Enforcement arbitration in a transition agreement, a rejected tentative agreement, and interest arbitration to create new contract terms are different mechanisms. The documented enforcement procedures and rejected agreements do not, by themselves, establish such a refusal. [82] [88] [89]

There is documented opposition in the prospective US Airways/American context. In December 2012, US Airways CWA-AFA leaders Roger Holmin and Deborah Volpe objected to applying an arbitration process to their members without a vote and criticized its bankruptcy-contract starting point. APFA’s October 2013 explanation of its strategy explicitly invoked the prolonged America West/US Airways negotiations and United’s continuing lack of a joint agreement. These were contemporaneous comparisons made by the participants, not merely later parallels. [90] [91] [92]

The 2014 US Airways/American protocol, agreed by management, APFA, and CWA-AFA, provided a materially different endpoint. Failure to reach or ratify a tentative agreement sent unresolved issues to final and binding interest arbitration. The agreement specified aggregate market value and separate aggregate protections for each legacy group, rather than preserving every individual provision. It did not retain a second membership vote or company veto over the award. The US Airways members’ approval of the process was distinct from later rejection of the combined tentative agreement. [93] [94] [95]

After rejection of the 2014 tentative agreement, the December 13 award adopted the TA except for its wage scale and rejected specified additional requests. On December 18, however, management agreed to restore the TA wages effective January 1, 2015—the date the lower award schedule was to start. APFA’s contemporary announcement identified the top-rate increase from $50.17 to $53.52. The lower awarded wage scale consequently did not remain the five-year wage schedule; the later restoration changed that outcome. [96] [97] [98]

This comparison exposes a real tradeoff: a binding endpoint can reduce open-ended bargaining but constrain the result and remove a later veto; a recommendation subject to approval preserves that choice but does not ensure settlement. The US Airways/American comparator traces the agreement, award, wage restoration, and later implementation. The two processes offered different degrees of finality and different constraints on the result. [93] [77]

15. Common contracts, unequal retirement legacies

The clearest comparison is between earned benefits, future benefit earning, and replacement contributions. United and US Airways participants could retain PBGC-administered payments while losing additional accrual under their terminated company plans. American participants retained sponsor-maintained accrued obligations through a freeze but also lost future accrual. Eligible Continental participants continued in CARP; Micronesia’s IAM pension was a separate arrangement. These are parallel legacy disparities, not proof of identical financial losses. [8] [10] [11] [9] [2]

The same legacy divide is examined in the US Airways/American report’s pension comparison: American’s frozen, sponsor-maintained pension obligations are distinguished from the terminated US Airways plan administered by PBGC. Continental’s continuing CARP accrual is a different outcome; the common issue is the persistence of legacy retirement differences under one employer.

Preservation of one group’s pension did not itself compensate another for earlier losses. Nor would eliminating the retained pension be a remedy for those losses. A common future contribution could improve retirement security without recreating past accrual, early-retirement subsidies, benefit forms, or lost years of employer funding. The relevant comparison is the full arrangement for a defined employee cohort, not a label such as “pension” or a single 401(k) percentage.

This puts subsequent bargaining decisions alongside the original bankruptcy outcomes. Preserving Continental benefits was consequential; so were the questions of access for other employees, recognition of prior service, contribution improvements, and whether a feasible new plan could address continuing differences. The record does not establish that union identity alone caused the bankruptcy results, but that does not remove management’s and the representative’s later choices from scrutiny. The pension reference explains the separate plan types and legal mechanisms. [14] [2] [46]

From 2016 LOA 9 to 2026 LOA 4

The 2016 JCBA’s LOA 9 required United and CWA-AFA to meet and address whether developing a new, cost-neutral defined-benefit plan was practical, while expressly not requiring either party to agree to implementation. [2]

In the United 2026–2031 CBA, LOA 4 — DB Plan Discussion (LOA 4-1–4-2; PDF pages 291–292) carries forward that framework. It requires the Company and the Union not merely to meet, but to reach agreement on whether development of a new cost-neutral defined-benefit plan is practical. Neither party is required to agree to implementation. The letter runs concurrently with the 2026–2031 agreement and is subject to its duration and amendment provisions. [99]

That feasibility undertaking is distinct from the letters preserving CARP for existing eligible participants. It is not a restoration order, adoption of a new pension, or an entitlement to credited past service. The section-by-section analysis of LOA 4 explains its relationship to the existing retirement provisions and the difference between a required discussion and an implemented benefit.

There were pension deliberations outside those letters. A 2015 CWA-AFA board action directed a re-examination of the CWA/ITU pension option and contemplated a report upon favorable completion. An August 2025 benefits update described a meeting about existing retirement programs’ investment strategy, oversight, and administration. Neither establishes the outcome of the distinct new-plan feasibility undertaking. [100] [101]

No final LOA-specific feasibility determination is identified in these public documents. They do not disclose the actuarial assumptions, the cost-neutrality baseline, or how proposed costs would be allocated among groups. That leaves the outcome of the undertaking unresolved in the public account; it does not establish that the required discussions never occurred. [99] [101] [100]

16. What the law protects—and what it does not automatically equalize

Federal law protects pension rights and regulates benefit discrimination, but it does not generally require identical retirement arrangements solely because employees now work for the same merged airline. Labor Department disclosure regulations expressly contemplate different benefit classes. That recognition does not excuse discrimination prohibited by other laws or noncompliance with the plan’s actual promises. [102] [103]

Earned benefits and plan mergers. ERISA’s anti-cutback rule generally protects accrued pension benefits against reduction by amendment, subject to statutory qualifications. Protection of already-earned benefits is not an unconditional guarantee of the same future accrual formula. Section 204(h) also addresses notice of significant future-accrual reductions. A pension-plan merger under § 208 protects each participant’s own before-and-after entitlement under its hypothetical-termination test; it does not compare one legacy employee’s benefit with another’s or automatically admit every acquired employee to an existing plan. A corporate merger is not necessarily a pension-plan merger. [104] [105]

Fair representation. A union’s duty in bargaining prohibits arbitrary, discriminatory, or bad-faith conduct, while allowing substantial latitude in difficult negotiations. In Air Line Pilots Association v. O’Neill, the Supreme Court applied that standard to a settlement arising from Continental’s pilot strike—not a flight-attendant pension ruling. Different outcomes for employee groups are not automatically a breach. The meaningful inquiry concerns the contemporaneous alternatives, rational consideration of the represented groups’ interests, and good faith—not whether hindsight identifies a more attractive bargain. [106] [107]

Promises, interference, and communications. ERISA provides routes to enforce benefits due under plan terms and qualifying equitable relief; § 510 prohibits specified interference with attainment or exercise of benefit rights. The law also distinguishes designing a plan from administering it as a fiduciary. Varity v. Howe concerned misleading benefits communications in a fiduciary capacity; Hughes Aircraft v. Jacobson explains why plan-design decisions are not automatically fiduciary acts. Those distinctions matter to reliance claims, but they do not establish wrongdoing in the United retiree-medical dispute without its specific evidence and procedural history. [108] [109] [110] [111]

Protected-characteristic discrimination. The Equal Pay Act, Title VII, ADEA, and ADA address compensation and benefits discrimination on specified protected grounds. Prior-airline status alone is not one of those listed characteristics. A legacy classification could nevertheless require scrutiny if it masks prohibited discrimination or has a legally actionable discriminatory effect. Age and pension-service status are not interchangeable; a pension distinction may be relevant to age discrimination without proving it by itself. Tax-law coverage and nondiscrimination requirements also are not a general equal-pension mandate for every bargaining group. [112] [103] [113] [114]

State protections and federal boundaries. California’s Equal Pay Act, for example, addresses sex-, race-, and ethnicity-based compensation differences for substantially similar work, including benefits. It does not create a pension-parity right based solely on predecessor-carrier identity. ERISA preemption and, for airlines, the Railway Labor Act’s distinction between independent legal rights and contract-interpretation disputes require separate analysis. State protections are neither universally displaced nor automatically available to rewrite an ERISA plan. [115] [116] [117] [118]

This framework is general legal context, not a determination that any party in these histories violated a duty or that a particular claim remains actionable. Historical conduct requires the law and operative documents applicable at the time, as well as consideration of remedies, procedures, prior judgments, and deadlines. Legal permissibility and the economic adequacy of a negotiated solution remain different questions.

Conclusion

United/Continental’s flight-attendant integration was not one event. Corporate closing, representation, subsidiary improvements, joint bargaining, ratification, contractual effectiveness, and common scheduling each changed different parts of the employment relationship. A common wage table eventually replaced three predecessor pay systems; implementation and legacy benefits required further distinctions. [1] [60] [2] [3]

The comparison with America West/US Airways demonstrates that common union representation alone did not guarantee a prompt joint agreement. The later US Airways/American experience shows how a binding endpoint could change that process, while the United 2015 proposal shows why a similarly described but nonbinding mechanism required separate evaluation. Bargaining choices, member decisions, economic standards, and implementation obligations—not elapsed time alone—explain the differences. [83] [93] [77]

The retirement comparison remains consequential after common contracts arrive. Preserving one legacy group’s pension can coexist with another group’s unrepaired losses. Recognizing that disparity does not require denying PBGC-protected payments, calling American’s freeze continued accrual, or assuming that all alternatives were feasible. It requires distinguishing what employees had earned, what they could earn next, what replacements they received, and what commitments management and the union undertook to address the remaining divide. A14. Sick and occupational leave banks

Agreement Provision and practical distinction
United at closing Four sick hours per month, up to 950. Reserve sick charges varied by month length. Specified workers’ compensation benefits could be supplemented with sick credit. [6]
Continental at closing Four sick and four occupational-injury hours monthly at the forty-paid-hour threshold; two plus two at twenty to under forty. Caps 1,000 sick / 400 occupational, with a separate catastrophic bank and conditional rapid reaccrual. [5]
Micronesia at closing Separate four-hour sick and occupational monthly accruals, with a fifty-paid-hour condition when pay status changed during the month. Caps 1,000 / 400 and a separate catastrophic bank. Continental’s twenty/forty-hour rule did not automatically apply. [7]
2016 JCBA, as adopted Four hours monthly in each sick and occupational category, credited quarterly under the 120 / sixty-hour conditions. Caps 1,250 sick / 400 occupational. Transition balances and use conditions remained important. [2]

A15. Personal, medical and parental leave

Agreement Provision and practical distinction
United at closing Personal/educational leave up to 180 days with qualifying ninety-day extensions. Seniority accrual varied by leave type; medical leave allowed up to three years. Written adoptive and paternal leave provisions were separate from statutory rights. [6]
Continental at closing Generally unpaid. Company-offered and personal leave accrued all-purpose seniority for the first ninety days, then specified bidding/pass purposes. Medical leave was limited by disability, active service, and a six-year maximum. Nonprobationary parental leave generally ninety days, with discretionary extensions. [5]
Micronesia at closing Generally unpaid. Company-offered leave accrued all-purpose seniority throughout; personal leave had the ninety-day distinction. Medical leave used the disability/service/six-year limits. This company-offered treatment differed from Continental’s. [7]
2016 JCBA, as adopted Section 15 consolidated leave provisions. Eligibility, seniority, benefit continuation, statutory rights, and exceptions prevent a single numerical ranking of all leave categories. [2]

A16. Active and retiree medical benefits

Agreement Provision and practical distinction
United at closing Active PPO contribution reached 20% in 2008, with specified contribution-growth limits from 2009; the book listed $250 / $500 deductibles. Pre-cutoff retirees, later retirees, and active employees had different rules. [6]
Continental at closing Companywide plans with notice and explanation of changes. LOA 18 capped aggregate projected flight-attendant premiums at 20% of projected coverage cost, not each individual’s cost. Ages sixty to sixty-five bridge-medical provisions used sick-bank exchange and separate contribution options. [5]
Micronesia at closing Company benefit programs and notice provisions, with a separate age-sixty-to-sixty-five bridge-medical arrangement. Actual premiums and full cost-sharing schedules require the applicable plan documents. [7]

A17. Retirement participation and credit

Agreement Provision and practical distinction
United at closing Post-termination defined contribution: employer direct contributions reached 3% by 2008 or 2009 according to hire cohort, plus a dollar-for-dollar match of the first 3%. This was not continued accrual in the terminated pension. [6] [119]
Continental at closing CARP eligibility and specified earlier-service credit, plus LOA 27’s separate pension-choice and fallback provisions. Retention for covered employees did not make United or Micronesia employees CARP participants. [5]

A18. Merger scope, seniority and furlough protections

Agreement Provision and practical distinction
United at closing Customary flight-attendant work protection and a preserved 1986 scope letter addressing controlled successor, subsidiary, and alter-ego operations, with exceptions. These were not the later fleet-allocation fence. [6]
Continental at closing Successor assumption; fair-and-equitable seniority integration; separate inflight operations/no interchange without consent pending integration and a contract; no merger-caused furlough during the specified interval. Expedited grievance arbitration enforced those duties. [5]

A19. Training compensation, scheduling and rest

Agreement Provision and practical distinction
United at closing $9 per training/meeting hour, with one- or three-hour event minima under specified conditions. Removed-trip protection and long-distance training-travel rest rules were separate. [6]
Continental at closing Initial new-equipment and recurrent training reached 2:30 per day by December 30, 2009. The recurrent-training daily-pay provision covered a maximum of two days per calendar year; it was not a limit on training attendance. Home study and computer-based training assignments each paid one hour, with separate dropped-trip protection and training-travel rules. [5]
Micronesia at closing At closing, initial/recurrent training paid 2:15 per day, rising to 2:30 December 30, 2010. A20. Disability coverage and limitations

Agreement Provision and practical distinction
United at closing LTD eligibility after one year; automatic enrollment with opt-out and payroll deductions. Continental at closing Contractual benefit-program and medical-leave protections; the complete contemporaneous LTD formula and individual election terms are not established by the cited CBA alone. [5]

Appendix B. Terms, abbreviations, and document types

Organizations and contract terms. CWA-AFA is the Association of Flight Attendants within the Communications Workers of America; sources from before the affiliation use AFA. IAM is the International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers; UFA was the Union of Flight Attendants. APFA represents American flight attendants. CARP is the Continental Airlines Retirement Plan; IAM NPP is the IAM National Pension Plan. NMB means National Mediation Board. CBA means collective bargaining agreement; JCBA means joint CBA; TA means tentative agreement; LOA means letter of agreement. A lineholder holds an awarded flying schedule; a reserve is available for assignments. A rig is a minimum paid-credit formula based on duty or trip time. An ID is the legacy-United term for a trip pairing. ISM refers to Continental/Micronesia’s inflight-service-manager position.

Chapter 11 and contract rejection. Reorganization does not by itself rewrite every labor agreement. § 1113 governs the process for rejection of covered CBAs; Continental’s 1983 case preceded that statute. § 1114 separately governs specified retiree insurance benefits. [18] [33] [17]

Defined benefit, defined contribution, and PBGC. A defined-benefit plan specifies a pension formula; a defined-contribution arrangement specifies contributions to accounts whose eventual value depends on funding and investment results. A freeze and a termination are not equivalent. PBGC’s 2005 notice explains the historical guarantee framework relevant here. [41] [43]

Accrued benefit, vesting, and credited service. The benefit already earned, the right to retain it, and the service used to calculate it are related but distinct. The crossover provision illustrates how prior service can count for vesting without increasing CARP credited service. [14] [58]

Interest arbitration and grievance arbitration. Interest arbitration determines new contract terms under the agreed or applicable procedure; grievance arbitration interprets or enforces existing obligations. A nonbinding recommendation is not a binding award. [93] [5] [77]

NMB single-system determination and certification. The flight-attendant representation decisions established the appropriate combined craft/class and its representative; they did not themselves negotiate common wages, integrate every seniority or scheduling process, or terminate pension plans. [1] [60]
